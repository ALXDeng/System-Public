Heat and health · Evidence map

Which Medicines Make a Heat Wave More Dangerous, and What Prescribers Can Change

By the System Public Challenge team, Visualize 3Evidence from the System Public Graph, FDA labels, CDC and NYC Health

The evidence is scattered across pharmacology, epidemiology and climate research, and very little of it measures a drug and heat together. So this map grades every claim. Shading shows how sure we are, never how dangerous a drug is. A finding counts as strong only when several heat-exposure studies agree and at least one is in System's curated (vetted) release.

The map sorts each drug class by the four things a prescriber can actually do before or during a heat wave: adjust the dose, hold or switch, monitor more closely, or store it differently.

Guidance gaps

The warnings point at the wrong drugs

Heat guidance and drug labels lean on mechanism: which drugs dull sweating or drain fluids. Measured outcomes tell a partly different story. Lithium and anticholinergics are flagged nearly everywhere, but the heat studies that exist found no clear effect. Meanwhile insulin, opioids, SGLT2 inhibitors and stimulants show measured or plausible harm and barely register.

Storage and stability

Some products fail in the heat before the patient does

The research graph has almost nothing on drug stability, though PubMed lists hundreds of papers on it. What exists comes from labels. The thresholds are low: an epinephrine auto-injector is labeled for a room no warmer than 25 °C, a temperature New York passes most summer afternoons.

Wearables

Where the evidence runs out

Could a watch or sensor personalize heat advice for someone on these drugs? The physiology is well studied, and there is a real literature on wearables and heat stress. But the evidence narrows fast, and almost nothing tests a wearable in people taking a heat-sensitive medication.
